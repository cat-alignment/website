Research agendas in theoretical AI safety

Last updated: November 2026.

Our non-exhaustive classification of the main theoretical AI safety agendas, with the maths each uses and representative organisations.

New agendas. We think finding new agendas would be really valuable, and we see it as one of the main ways Cambridge mathematicians can contribute.

Assumptions. For every agenda, we think it is worth asking what assumptions it rests on, and how it could fail when those assumptions don’t hold in practice. So for each one we note where we think it can fail.

1. Game theory and protocols

ShowHide ↓

Mostly scalable oversight: treat the model as a bounded adversary, set up a game, and prove that its equilibria have the property we want, such as honesty. Examples: debate, prover–verifier games, and open-source game theory (agents that can read each other’s code).

Fails when
the game’s assumptions don’t hold, or training doesn’t find the desired equilibrium.
Maths
complexity theory, game theory.
Who
Resolution (debate).

2. Learning theory

ShowHide ↓

2.1 Top-down

Prove theorems over a whole model class.

Fails when
the proof needs simplifying assumptions that real architectures or learning processes break, or when its quantities can only be estimated.
Maths
algebraic geometry, Bayesian statistics, statistical physics.
Who
Resolution, including the singular learning theory agenda of the former Timaeus team; PIRAMID (Principles of Intelligence).

2.2 Bottom-up

Derive training dynamics in closed form for solvable toy networks, then check real networks match.

Fails when
the phenomenon changes qualitatively at scale.
Maths
differential equations, random matrix theory, statistical physics.
Who
mostly academic groups, for example Andrew Saxe (UCL), Daniel Kunin and Jamie Simon (UC Berkeley) (our current understanding; we’ll check when we watch their lectures on MISO day 5).

3. Agent foundations

ShowHide ↓

3.1 Unbounded agents

Prove things about AIXI-style idealised agents (agents based on Solomonoff induction).

Fails when
systems built from LLMs don’t resemble those idealisations.
Maths
logic, decision theory, algorithmic information theory.
Who
AIXI Labs.

3.2 Bounded agents

The same, but with computational limits built in (the learning-theoretic agenda, logical induction).

Fails when
the bounds are vacuous at scale, or the chosen limitations aren’t the ones that matter.
Maths
logic, decision theory, probability theory.
Who
CORAL, Dovetail, Resolution’s agent foundations team, XOR Labs.

4. Mechanistic estimation (heuristic arguments)

ShowHide ↓

Estimate how often a model shows rare but unacceptable behaviour from the model’s weights or learning process rather than from samples.

Fails when
[we (CAT) do not know yet].
Maths
probability theory, complexity theory.
Who
ARC.

5. Interpretability

ShowHide ↓

Inspect a model’s weights and try to interpret them. Mostly empirical, but with some theoretical results.

Fails when
the network’s computation doesn’t break into human-understandable parts, or explanations cover only a small part of its behaviour.
Maths
linear algebra, high-dimensional geometry.
Who
Simplex (theory); Goodfire, Anthropic and Google DeepMind interpretability teams (empirical).

Iliad has research teams across nearly all of these agendas: debate, learning theory, agency, heuristic arguments, and the geometry and structure of neural networks, plus data attribution.

Other lists of directions and open problems

MAISI describes some of these areas in its research directions: roughly open-source game theory (1), mechanistic estimation (4) and interpretability (5) from the list above.

For concrete problems, also see Math for AI Safety: An invitation for mathematicians by Lionel Levine, a survey organised by field, with an open problem at the end of each section. It grew into the MAIS repository, a much longer list of open problems. Our understanding is that this list was largely generated by AI and may not have been fully checked by humans.
